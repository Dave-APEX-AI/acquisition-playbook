Acquisition Memo · No. 05 — The Approach
Individual-operator play · 14 Sep 2026
How we reach owners — and what we say

The Approach

The whole idea in one line

You're not pitching a deal — you're offering someone's life's work a safe pair of hands. Lead with their legacy and their people, never with "I want to buy your business."

01

Why this works — and why you win as first-timers

These owners are 55+, no successor, and haven't decided to sell. Their deepest fear is handing 20 years of work to a faceless fund that strips it and sacks the staff. Two real people who'll run it themselves is the exact opposite — and your honesty is an asset, not a weakness.

Legacy & continuity
What they built keeps going, with their name intact.
Their people are safe
The team they care about is looked after, not cut.
Real, local, hands-on
Human beings who'll be in the business — not a spreadsheet.
Discretion & no pressure
Confidential, unhurried, easy to say no to.
Genuine intent
You want to own and grow it, not flip it in three years.
Honest > slick
"First time, doing it properly" beats a polished predator.
The honesty guardrail

Every word must be true. You're serious first-time buyers with the funding route identified but not yet arranged — so the copy below never claims cash is ready or that you've done this before. Add a funding line only once it's genuinely in place.

02

Best practice — do & don't

Do
  • Address the owner by name; sign with real names
  • Give one genuine, specific reason their firm stood out
  • Keep it short — under ~150 words, a real person's voice
  • Make it easy to reply — phone and email
  • Promise confidence and mean it
  • One soft ask: "would you ever consider…"
  • Post a physical letter first — it beats email for this age group
Don't
  • Mention price, valuation or an offer — far too soon
  • Use "synergies / portfolio / acquire / EBITDA" — broker-speak
  • Sound mass-mailed — no "Dear Business Owner"
  • Manufacture urgency or pressure
  • Claim funding or experience you don't have
  • Chase more than twice — respect a silence
  • Lead with yourself — lead with them
03

The letter — your main move

Posted, addressed to the named owner. Fill the [amber] parts per company — the specific reason is what stops it feeling like a mailshot.

Letter to the owner~140 words · post it

Dear [Owner first name],

I'll keep this short and personal. My business partner and I are looking to buy and personally run one established [cleaning / electrical / facilities] business — not to flip it or strip it back, but to take it on, look after the team, and build on what someone has spent years creating.

[Company] stood out to us — [one true, specific reason: e.g. "you've clearly built something solid and well-run over 20-odd years"]. I've no idea whether stepping back has ever crossed your mind, and there's no assumption here — but if it ever might, we'd value a quiet, no-obligation conversation.

I'll be straight: this would be our first acquisition, so we'd go about it respectfully and properly, and we'd treat anything you share in complete confidence.

If it's not for you, I completely understand and won't trouble you again. If you're open to a chat, you can reach me on [phone] or [email].

With respect,
[Your name] & [Partner name]
[phone] · [email]

Email & LinkedIn — where you have them

05

The phone opener

If you have a number, a warm call after the letter converts best. Say who you are, disarm fast, make the ask, give them an easy out.

Cold / follow-up call — first 20 secondssay it, then listen

"Hi [name], my name's [X] — you don't know me, and I'll be quick so you can tell me to get lost. I'm not a broker and I'm not selling anything.

My business partner and I are looking to buy and run one [sector] business ourselves, and yours is one we really rated. I just wondered — and no pressure at all — whether you'd ever consider passing it on one day to someone who'd genuinely look after it and the team?"

→ Then stop talking. Let them react. If curious → move toward a proper chat (Part 3). If not → "completely understand, thanks for your time," and leave the door open.

Before you dial — one legal step

Screen any business number against the CTPS (Corporate Telephone Preference Service) before cold-calling — calling a registered number can be an ICO offence. Numbers a business publishes for enquiries are generally fine; when in doubt, letter/email first.

06

The sequence — respectful, never a pest

DAY 0
Post the letter
Personalised, to the named owner. This is the main event.
DAY 10–14
One follow-up
A warm call if you have a number, or a two-line "just checking my note reached you." That's it — never a third chase.
NO REPLY
Let it rest → nurture
Silence is an answer. Move them to a light list and revisit in 6–12 months — many owners sell 1–3 years after first being asked.
A REPLY
Book a proper conversation
Warm, curious, no numbers yet — that's the First Call (Part 3, coming next).
The one metric that matters

Expect low reply rates (a few %) — that's normal and fine. You need a handful of genuine conversations, not a flood. One warm "yes, tell me more" from 200 letters is a win.
