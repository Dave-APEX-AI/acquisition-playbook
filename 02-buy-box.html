The mandate in one line
A boring, essential, manager-run UK business making £250k–£500k a year, whose 55+ owner has no successor — one that keeps earning after the owner walks out the door, because that owner is who we're replacing.

Every criterion below flows from two anchors: it must be fundable under the £2m GGS cap, and it must survive losing its owner. That second test is doing most of the work — the entire "keep the manager" strategy collapses if the business is the person. So we score owner-independence heavily, and — new in this version — we detect it from public data before we ever make contact (Section 03).

01 — Your sector steer (as asked)

Where to aim — and where familiarity is a trap

You picked "all of the above, give me your steer." Here it is, ranked by fit to this specific model (remote, keep-the-manager, debt-serviced), not by how interesting the business is. The uncomfortable part is at the bottom.

Tier 1 · Hunt here

Facilities & maintenance services

The best fit, full stop. Revenue is contracted and recurring, the work is unglamorous and essential, customers are diversified, and it's the least owner-dependent category — the value sits in the contract book and the crew, not the founder's genius. Also the most fragmented, which is ideal for bolting on a second one later.

Look for: commercial cleaning · grounds/landscaping maintenance · M&E and building maintenance · statutory testing & inspection (fire, electrical/PAT, lift, HVAC servicing) · pest control · waste & hygiene.

Tier 1 · Hunt here

Contract-based trades & installation

This is where your trades knowledge is a genuine edge — you can tell a real contracting business from a one-man band swinging the hammer. The rule: only ones with planned/recurring commercial contracts, engineers on PAYE, and a project manager already running the diary. Buy the servicing annuity, not the founder's tool bag.

Look for: commercial electrical & mechanical contractors · fire & security install + maintenance · HVAC / refrigeration service companies · plumbing & heating with maintenance contracts.

Tier 2 · Selective

B2B distribution & niche manufacturing

The classic "silver-tsunami factory." Genuinely fundable and often manager-run — but heavier on stock, capex and customer concentration, which raises diligence and working-capital demands. Favour distribution (capital-light, sticky supplier/customer relationships) over deep manufacturing (old plant, reinvestment, cyclicality). Great targets exist here; just underwrite harder.

Look for: specialist wholesale/distribution · niche branded product manufacture · sub-assembly & engineering with a repeat customer base and modern kit.

Tier 3 · Familiarity trap

Recruitment & staffing

You know it best — which is exactly why I'd hold you back. For this model it's the weakest fit: the assets walk out the door every night, billings are relationship- and cycle-dependent, and a downturn evaporates revenue you're paying debt against. "Keep the manager, show up quarterly" fails hardest here, because in recruitment the people are the business.

Only if: it's a temp/contract desk with recurring weekly margin (not perm placement), a locked-in team on real handcuffs, and repeat framework clients — otherwise pass, however well you know the trade.

The steer, distilled

Spend 70% of your search time in Tier 1 (facilities/maintenance + contract trades), 25% opportunistically in Tier 2 distribution, and treat recruitment as opportunistic-only with the guardrails above. Buy recurring revenue and a team, not a person.

03 — The owner-dependence test

Is it a business, or is it just them?

The one question that makes or breaks this whole strategy. The good news: you don't have to wait for a meeting to get a strong read — several proxies are visible in public data before you make contact, so we triage the list first and spend outreach time only on the ones that pass.

The signal you named — and it's the cheapest to automate

The eponymous-name test. Does the company name contain the owner-director's own name? "D. Murphy Electrical", "J Smith & Sons", "Karen Booth Associates" → the business is likely the person's brand, so the goodwill may walk with them. A neutral trading name — "Northgate Facilities", "Apex Mechanical Services" → a healthier sign it stands on its own. We match each director's name against the company name automatically and flag the hits.

Layer A · Automatable

Built straight into the Phase 2 list

Computed for every target from free Companies House data, producing an owner-dependence risk score per company:

Eponymous name? director's name inside the company name → personal-brand flag.
Management depth? count of active directors + a company secretary + years of appointment history — a lone director with no other officers scores worst.
Employee count? "average employees" from filed accounts — 15 staff behind £400k profit is a machine; 2 staff behind £400k profit is one irreplaceable person.
Maturity? incorporated 15–30+ yrs ago is likelier to have real processes than a 4-year-old founder shop.

Layer B · Light enrichment

On the shortlist, semi-automatic

Once the list is narrowed, a quick pass over each survivor's public footprint:

Website: a real brand with a "meet the team" page and named managers — or is it all "I" and the founder's face?
LinkedIn: is there a named General / Operations Manager, or is the owner the only profile attached to the firm?
Reviews: do Google/Trustpilot reviews name the owner personally — "John always sorts us" — i.e. owner-delivered service?

Layer C · Human confirmation

The three questions that settle it (Phase 3)

No dataset proves independence — these questions on the first call do:

1. "Who runs the day-to-day when you're on holiday?"
2. "If you didn't come in for a month, what would actually break?"
3. "How many hours a week are you really in it — and doing what, exactly?"

Honest caveat

These are a triage filter, not a verdict. A neutral-named company can still be secretly owner-run, and an eponymous one can have a brilliant GM ready to step up. Layers A–B rank the list so you don't waste outreach; Layer C and full diligence deliver the actual answer.

04 — Businesses to definitely avoid

The walk-away list

These aren't "negotiate harder" flags — they're hard exclusions. Any one of them present, you pass, because each one breaks a load-bearing assumption of the strategy.

Kills "keep the manager"

The owner is the business

Sales run on their personal relationships, they're the technical expert, or their name is over the door. It decapitates the day they leave. Fatal for a remote owner.

Kills the debt cover

Customer concentration

One client is 25%+ of revenue. Lose them and you can't service the loan. Common in small B2B — check the sales ledger, not the pitch.

Structural decline

A shrinking or disrupted sector

Print, high-street retail, anything being eaten by software or offshoring. You'd be servicing fixed debt against a falling top line.

Scheme ineligible

Distressed or loss-making

GGS requires a viable business "not in difficulty." Turnarounds are a different (much riskier) game — not this one, not for a first deal.

Hidden liabilities

Balance-sheet landmines

Defined-benefit pension deficits, unpaid tax, live litigation, environmental/contamination risk (real on old factory sites), big dilapidations.

Cash drain

Capex treadmill

Constant reinvestment just to stand still — ageing plant, fleet, or tech. Cash that should service debt gets eaten by machines.

Fake profit

Lifestyle books & heavy add-backs

Profit only appears after aggressive "add-backs," or vanishes once you pay a real manager's salary. Cash-heavy trades with vague records: walk.

Single point of failure

One irreplaceable employee or licence

A sole key engineer, or a personal/non-transferable licence, accreditation or lease the business can't operate without.

Property risk

Toxic lease or premises

Short lease with no security of tenure, imminent rent review, onerous repairing obligations, or a site you can't move.

Outside your circle

Anything you can't explain simply

Fashion, fads, speculative tech, or a model you don't understand. First deal: buy boring and buy what you can grasp cold.

05 — The 60-second screen

Pass/fail before you waste a call

Before any deeper look, a target must clear all of these. One "no" and it's out of the pipeline.

Every box must be ticked to proceed
  • Manager-run? Is there a named person who already runs it day-to-day — not the owner?
  • Profit in range? Roughly £250k–£500k, and stable or growing over 3 years?
  • Owner motivated? 55+, no obvious successor, plausibly open to selling and leaving money in?
  • Diversified? No single customer dominating; recurring or repeat revenue?
  • Essential & boring? Demand that doesn't switch off in a downturn or get disrupted?
  • Fundable? Likely price under ~£1.8m so GGS + seller loan + investor can cover it?
  • No red flags? None of the walk-away list above is obviously present?
