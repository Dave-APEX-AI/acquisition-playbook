The working reference behind the Scorecard

The Rule Book

The judgment behind every score: how to read a company off Companies House and its accounts before you ever speak to anyone, what "good" and "bad" actually look like sector by sector, the numbers worked through, and the traps that catch first-time buyers.

The rule, in one line

Buy a boring, essential business that already runs without its owner — proven profit we can grow, at a price the business itself repays. If it's really just the owner, walk.

01

The portrait — what "perfect" looks like

A 15–30-year-old Ltd in facilities, maintenance or contract trades, run day-to-day by a named manager and a PAYE team while a 58+ owner with no successor has drifted to two days a week. It earns £250k–£500k a year off contracted, repeat revenue spread across dozens of customers — none over 15–20%. The name is a brand, not the owner's surname. Accounts are filed on time, show rising retained profit and real cash, little debt, and light capex. It's never been marketed and prices haven't moved in years — so the growth is sitting there for us. Priced around 3× profit (≤£1.8m), the seller will leave money in and hand over properly.

Use the portrait as your yardstick

You will rarely find all of it. Score how far a target falls short, and whether the shortfalls are fixable (under-marketed = upside) or fatal (owner-dependent = walk).

02

Reading Companies House — before you speak to anyone

Everything below is free and public. Learn to read it and you disqualify 80% of targets in minutes.

Officers
Sole director, no secretary → owner-dependent. Two-three directors incl. a non-family manager → delegated, good. A 30s–40s director sharing the founder's surname → succession planned internally, probably not for sale. A co-director who just resigned → a partnership split: opportunity or instability — ask.
PSC register
One person with 75%+ → clean control to buy. Several PSCs → more parties to align. A corporate PSC → a holding company sits above; understand the structure before you value it.
Filing history
Accounts on time every year → a disciplined going concern. Overdue / late / newly "dormant" → distress or an owner who's checked out — could be decay or a motivated seller, find out which.
Charges
An outstanding charge/debenture → existing bank or invoice-finance lending you'll refinance (and someone else holds security). A satisfied charge → debt paid off. None + healthy → unencumbered, the cleanest deal.
Incorporation date
15–30 yrs → reputation, likely systems, an older owner. Under ~8 yrs → founder-era, higher dependence risk.
SIC & office
One or two focused SIC codes → a real trading business. Many unrelated or property/holding codes → a lifestyle/SPV mix. Registered office at their own premises (not the accountant's) → more substance.
03

Reading the accounts — the cheat sheet

Small companies file "filleted" accounts — usually just a balance sheet, no profit figure. So you infer profit from the story the balance sheet tells across three years. Here's how to read it.

LineWhat it tells you
Retained earnings (P&L reserve)The single best signal. Rising year on year = the business makes and keeps profit. Falling = losses or the owner stripping cash out. Track the 3-year trend first.
Cash at bankLiquidity and how well it converts profit to cash. Healthy, stable cash = a real business. Shrinking cash + rising creditors = strain.
DebtorsMoney owed to them. Large vs turnover = slow payers or WIP tying up cash. A single huge debtor hints at customer concentration.
StockDistribution/mfg only. High = working capital tied up + obsolescence risk. Services should have ~none.
Creditors < 1yrTrade creditors, tax (VAT/PAYE/CT) and short-term debt. Compare to current assets → net current assets. Negative = possible cash-flow pressure.
Creditors > 1yrBank loans / long-term debt you'd inherit or refinance.
Director's loanIf the company owes the director → cash they'll want back on exit (affects price/structure). If the director owes the company → cash already extracted, sometimes parked as an "asset".
Fixed assetsPlant, vehicles, kit. High & ageing = a capex treadmill. Low = capital-light — ideal for a services buy.
Net assetsA rough value floor. Price above net assets = goodwill you're paying for — it must be justified by profit, and asset backing helps you borrow.
What you can't see — and must ask for

No turnover, gross margin or net profit is filed for small companies. Get 3 years of full accounts / management accounts from the owner or their accountant early — and a VAT-return sense-check of turnover. If they won't share after a good first meeting, that's a signal in itself.

04

Green flags & red flags — with the tell

Green — the "why" & the tell
  • Contracted recurring revenue — maintenance/servicing/framework language, steady debtors, repeat invoices.
  • Manager + team run it — named GM on the website/LinkedIn; owner already part-time.
  • Diversified customers — no single debtor dominating; many logos.
  • Under-priced / under-marketed — no marketing, prices flat for years = free upside.
  • Clean, cash-generative balance sheet — rising retained earnings, real cash, low debt.
  • Transferable accreditations — NICEIC, Gas Safe, ISO tied to the company, not the person.
  • Fragmented sector — lots of small rivals = bolt-on runway later.
Red — the "why" & the tell
  • The owner IS the business — eponymous name, sole director, reviews name them personally.
  • Customer concentration — one debtor is a big slice; "our biggest client" talk.
  • Structural decline / disruption — shrinking sector, tech or regulation about to bite.
  • Distressed — falling retained earnings, overdue filings, rising creditors. GGS-ineligible.
  • Add-back games — profit only appears after heroic "adjustments"; growing director's-loan extraction.
  • Capex treadmill / stock risk — big ageing fixed assets, or obsolete stock.
  • Key-man or lease landmine — one irreplaceable person; short lease; dilapidations; a personal licence.
05

Sector playbooks

The generic rules bend by sector. Where your value sits, and the sector-specific trap.

Facilities & maintenance

Value = the contract book + the crew
Good

Multi-year contracts, retained clients, low capex, recurring monthly revenue.

Bad

Wage-cost squeeze (min-wage rises), high staff churn, one big contract (an NHS trust, a council).

Upside

Cross-sell services, raise under-priced contracts, add planned maintenance.

Contract trades — electrical / mechanical / fire / HVAC

Value = engineers + the servicing base + accreditations
Good

Planned + reactive maintenance contracts, PAYE engineers, a service manager running the diary.

Bad

Owner still on the tools; new-build/project reliant (cyclical, retentions, lumpy cash); accreditation tied to the owner.

Upside

Convert install customers to service contracts; raise labour rates; add compliance testing.

Distribution & niche manufacturing

Value = supplier rights + sticky customers + kit
Good

Exclusive distribution rights, repeat reorders, modern efficient kit.

Bad

Stock obsolescence, big working-capital swings, supplier/customer concentration, FX exposure, old machinery.

Upside

Pricing, an e-commerce channel, range extension, tighter buying.

The lesson in one line

At a £1.2m price (≈3.4×) that deal has a comfortable 2× cover. Pay £1.6m (≈4.6×) for the same profit and cover falls toward 1.5× — one bad quarter and you're squeezed. The multiple you pay is the margin of safety you keep. Never buy a deal that only works at its best case.

07

The traps that catch first-time buyers

Add-back inflation
Sellers pad "adjustments" to lift the profit you're pricing off. Accept only genuine, provable, non-recurring add-backs.
WIP / retentions hiding cash
In trades, unbilled work and held-back retentions can flatter the balance sheet. Ask what's really collectable.
The owner's relationships
If the big customers stay because of them, revenue can walk on completion. Test how contracts are actually held.
Working-capital swing
A debtor-heavy or seasonal business may need a cash injection just to keep running post-deal. Fund for it.
Dilapidations & leases
End-of-lease repair bills and rent reviews are real liabilities that never show on the headline price.
Earn-out misalignment
A seller staying on an earn-out may optimise for the short term. Structure it around what you actually want built.
When to walk — no exceptions

Owner is the business · one customer dominates · declining sector · distressed · hidden liabilities · we can't name two ways to grow it · the numbers only work at best case · or the gut says it's only cheap because something's wrong. A cheap price never rescues a bad business.
