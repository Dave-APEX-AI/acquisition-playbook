The Reality Check

Read this when you're most keen

Everything else here is built to make you eager. This memo exists to keep you honest: expect it to take a year-plus, refuse happy ears, and be truthful now about who runs it and how often you're in the UK.

01

The real timeline — it takes longer than you think

A first acquisition is a marathon. Every stage slips, sellers go cold, and a good share of searches never close a first deal. Plan for the long version.

Weeks 0–6
Get ready
Buy box, advisers lined up, funding route understood. The easy part.
Month 1–6+
Sourcing & outreach
The hardest, longest phase. Hundreds of letters → a handful of real conversations. Runs continuously in the background the whole way.
Per live target
Calls → Heads of Terms
Weeks to a couple of months to build trust and agree an outline — if they're a real seller.
1–3 months
Due diligence
Where deals most often die. Slow, detailed, and where surprises surface.
4–10 weeks
Legals + funding → completion
Almost always slips. Lenders and lawyers take longer than promised.
Realistic total
12–18 months to a first completed deal
Some do it in 6–9 with luck; many take 2 years; a large share stop before closing one.
The emotional timeline nobody warns you about

You will fall for a business and watch it collapse in diligence, or the seller go cold after months. It stings. Don't bet emotionally on any single deal until it's signed — always keep other conversations alive so no one deal can break your morale or your judgement.

02

Don't get happy ears

"Happy ears" — hearing what you want to hear because you're keen to do a deal. It's the number-one way eager first-timers lose money. The story is always rosier than the numbers.

What you'll hear
What it really means
It basically runs itself
Until you prove it, assume it runs on them. Verify who actually covers holidays and quotes.
We're about to win a big contract
Price the business on what it earns today, never on a promise that may never land.
The staff will all stay
Get it in writing where you can, and meet the key people yourself. The manager is the whole deal.
Once you strip out my costs it makes loads
Add-backs are where sellers inflate profit. Accept only genuine, provable ones — the accountant checks.
Lots of interest, you'll need to move fast
Classic pressure (from broker or seller). Real deals survive you taking the time to do it properly.

The antidotes

  • Trust the numbers, not the narrative. Everything material is verified in diligence, or it doesn't count.
  • Write your walk-away lines before you fall for a business — the Scorecard's red flags — and hold them.
  • One of you is always the sceptic. Split the roles: one gets excited, the other pokes holes. Swap per deal.
  • Ask the killer question: "would I still buy this, at this price, if the seller's best case never happens?" If no — renegotiate or walk.
  • Patience is the edge. The discipline to pass on the first deal and wait for the right one is what separates winners.
03

Two of you, over and back to the UK — the operating reality

You'll own a real UK business with real staff, run by two people based mostly in Ireland. This is workable — the Common Travel Area and cheap short flights make it very doable — but only if you're honest now about how it actually runs.

The one thing everything rests on

The on-site manager is your anchor. A capable, retained second-in-command running day-to-day is what makes "over and back" possible. If they're strong and stay, the model works. If they're weak or walk, you're commuting constantly or moving. So the strength of that manager is your travel answer — vet it hard, lock them in with incentives, and have a plan for if they leave.

Split the roles — don't both do everything

Partner A · Inside

The operator

Closer to the business, more UK presence. Owns the manager relationship, operations, staff, customers. The face on the ground.

Partner B · Outside

The builder

Funding, finance, growth (the 5 Ways), investor relations, and sourcing the next acquisition. Keeps the wider plan moving.

Decide who's who before you buy. Two people both trying to run it whipsaws the staff and the manager.

Travel cadence — be honest about the front end

First 100 days
Heavy presence. One of you on-site most weeks (or extended stays). This is not a turn-up-once-a-quarter phase — you're earning the team's trust and learning the operation. Underestimate this and you'll break the business you just bought.
Months 3–12
Taper as it proves out. A steady rhythm — e.g. one partner over a fixed few days each week or fortnight — as the manager and systems earn your confidence.
Year 2+
Monthly / quarterly, if earned. The reel's "show up once a quarter" becomes real only after you've de-risked the business and proven the manager. It's the destination, not the start.
Coverage & the honest question

Never both go quiet. Agree who is "on" for the business each week so the manager always has one clear point of contact. Good reporting, cameras and cloud systems help you watch from Dublin — but early trust and problem-solving need face time, not WhatsApp.

The question to answer truthfully now: is one of you genuinely willing to near-commute or effectively relocate for the first 6–12 months? If neither is, the target has to be exceptionally manager-run (rare, and you'll pay a premium for it) — or this specific model is a stretch for you right now. Better to know that before you spend a penny than after.

04

What actually separates the ones who make it

The whole memo in five lines

1 · Patience — wait for the right business, don't force the first.
2 · Quality — buy something so resilient it never defaults.
3 · Discipline — don't overpay, don't over-leverage, hold your walk-away lines.
4 · A real operator with real bandwidth — decided and committed up front.
5 · Honesty — with the numbers, and with yourselves about the time it takes.

The Reality Check v1. The counterweight to the whole playbook — revisit it whenever a deal has you excited. None of this says don't do it; it says do it with your eyes open, slowly, and well.

Honest guidance, not financial advice. Every deal and every seller is different — verify everything.
